The week, and the fridge that forgets

Three of the premises behind the question are worth testing before costing anything. Two of them are wrong in your favour, and the third is right in a way that is worse than stated.

The short version: the weekly shopping list already exists and already works. The week is a cheap annotation on tables you have. The expensive half is the fridge — and not for the reason you would expect. It is expensive because every retrieval path in this database is a conjunctive filter, and a fridge is a ranking input. That mismatch is the whole cost.

Premises

What I checked, and what turned out to be true

You asked for corrections over agreement. Here are three, in order of how much they change the estimate.

Wrong — and this is the big one

“Shopping lists hold text rather than ingredient ids, which is a problem for aggregating a week.”

There are no shopping-list items at all. I grepped the whole migration set: no shopping_list_items, no line table, nothing. A shopping_lists row is (profile_id, recipe_id, name, menu_main_recipe_id, menu_title) — a list is a set of recipe references, and the lines are derived at read time by joining recipe_ingredients.

Which means the merge is already keyed on ingredient identity. mergeMenuIngredients in the client keys its map on ingredient.id, with the comment “the ingredient's own id, so one line stands for every dish that wants it.” There is no text matching anywhere in the path.

The aggregation you were about to spec is already shipped. EverythingToBuyScreen merges every recipe on every list into one shop: deduplicated by ingredient id, grouped by aisle from ingredients.category, scaled per-recipe by servings target, ticked by recipe_ingredients row id so one tick clears the item in every dish that wants it, and usable offline. It does not care whether the recipes behind it are three courses of one meal or twelve dishes across seven days.

Partly wrong — there is already a persistent memory

“The app holds no state between sessions — every open is a cold start where the user re-tells it what they have.”

True of the basket, false of the app. There are deliberately two ingredient stores. useIngredientStore is the basket — in memory, cleared on home focus, and the comment says that is correct and must stay. useRecentIngredientsStore is persisted to disk, capped at 12, described as “the ingredients this user actually reaches for, across sessions… the only thing in the picker that knows you have been here before.”

And it already draws the exact distinction the forgiving fridge needs. It refuses to record camera scans, because a scan writes eight items at once and would evict the list — “an inventory of what is in the fridge today, not what this cook keeps buying.” Somebody has already thought about staples-versus-today and picked the staples half.

Two real gaps, though: it is device-local AsyncStorage, not per-profile, so it does not survive a reinstall or reach a second device; and it is capped at 12 by design. It is a memory of habits, not an inventory.

Right — and worse than you said

“Menus are courses within a single meal, not meals across days, so there is no concept of a week.”

Correct, and I found no date anywhere: no planned_for, plan_date, day_of_week, scheduled_for or week_start column in any of the 84 migrations. The course vocabulary is closed at three — MENU_COURSE_SLOTS = ["appetizer", "side", "dessert"] — plus the main.

But the model does not merely fail to stretch. It actively fights a week, for a reason that would not show up until you shipped it: since 20260821000001, menus is shared community content, unique on (main_recipe_id, dish_signature). Two people who compose the same dishes get one row. Model a week as a menu and two users who plan the same five dinners silently share a plan — and unowned menus surface on the home feed and in the community sheet.

Two more structural refusals. A menu has a main; a week does not. And saved_menus is unique on (profile_id, main_recipe_id), so a profile could hold exactly one plan per main dish, forever.

Net effect on the estimate. The week gets cheaper than you assumed and the fridge gets dearer. If you were budgeting the plan as the big build and the fridge as a bolt-on, that is inverted.

Exhibit A

What happened to pantry_items, and whether the reason still applies

It was not dropped on a design objection. The drop migration says so in one line.

20260727000007_drop_pantry_items.sql
-- Remove the pantry_items feature (unused by client and backend). delete from cron.job where jobname = 'delete-expired-pantry-items'; drop table if exists pantry_items cascade;

The stated reason is disuse, not that inventory is a bad idea. That reason does not survive the feature being used. It is not a precedent against you.

What was there was more complete than the drop suggests. pantry_items was keyed (profile_id, ingredient_id) unique — ingredient ids, not text — with quantity, a unit_id FK, and expires_at. A before insert or update trigger computed the expiry from the ingredient's own shelf life. A delete_expired_pantry_items() function and a nightly pg_cron job at midnight UTC swept it.

The wall

Every retrieval path is a conjunctive filter, and a fridge is a ranking input

This is the real obstacle, and it is not where you were looking. It is not that the fridge is hard to store. It is that nothing here can consume one.

The ingredient filter in find_recipes is AND-all:

20260823000001_find_recipes_totals_facets.sql — line 140
(select count(distinct ri.ingredient_id) from recipe_ingredients ri where ri.ingredient_id = any (ingredients)) = array_length(ingredients, 1)

A recipe qualifies only if it contains every ingredient passed in. find_near_miss_recipes uses the identical clause at line 285. So does the suggestion half of the same function at line 334.

Point a fridge at that and it inverts: the more complete the inventory, the fewer results. Ten items returns almost nothing. Thirty returns zero, always. The failure mode is an empty feed that reads as a broken catalogue, and it gets worse precisely as the user invests more in the feature.

This is not a hypothetical. The repo already measured this exact trapdoor from the other direction. From 20260830000002_ingredient_use_count.sql: picking any of the eight unused tomato rows “empties the result outright: Gazpacho, which lists plain Tomato, is unreachable from a search for tomatoes” — and of 1041 ingredients, only ~345 are referenced by anything, so “two thirds of the picker is a trapdoor.” That migration sorted the picker to route around the hole. It did not close it.

What exists — filter

ingredients uuid[] → dish must contain all of them.

Correct for the picker, where the user names two or three things deliberately and means “both”.

Catastrophic for a fridge, where the list is long, passive, and partly wrong.

What a fridge needs — score

p_pantry uuid[] → order by overlap, coverage, and how few items are missing.

Nothing is excluded. A dish needing one thing you lack still appears, ranked lower, with the gap named.

Which is exactly the shape find_near_miss_recipes already returns for diets.

Do not overload the existing parameter. Its AND-all semantics are relied on by the fridge picker and the search screen's entity strip. A pantry score is a second, additive parameter, or a sibling function. Widening ingredients in place would silently change what every existing filter means.
The consolation is that this work pays for itself twice. An overlap-ranking path is the fix for the documented picker trapdoor as well as the precondition for the fridge. Even if you shelved the whole planner, this is a defensible piece of work on its own — which makes it the safest expensive thing on the list.

The forgiving fridge

Taking it as a constraint, not a feature

Your framing is right and the codebase already agrees with it in two places. The question is what it costs.

The design you described — infer from shopping and cooking, decay by shelf life, resync wholesale by photo, rank rather than filter — has a near-exact precedent here in profile_taste_signals: observed rather than declared, counted rather than logged, thresholded before it is acted on, written fire-and-forget through trackBackgroundTask so it never sits between the user and their first token. That is the contract a fridge writer should copy verbatim. It is proven and it is in this repo.

Taking your four properties in turn:

PropertyBuildableWhat it rests on, and where it gets expensive
Infer from what was shopped Partly The tick store is the obvious signal and it is ambiguous by construction. useIngredientCheckStore was deliberately merged from two stores — SHOPPING_CHECK_STORE and COOK_CHECKLIST_STORE — so one tick means “bought” or “measured out” and nothing distinguishes them. It is also device-local AsyncStorage, never sent to the server. Adding a shopped signal means either splitting that store again or inferring from the surface the tick happened on.
Infer from what was cooked New Cooking is not recorded anywhere. Finishing a cook currently deletes the recipe's shopping list (steps-finish.tsx) — which is the closest thing to a consumption event in the app, and it is thrown away rather than emitted. The cheap version is to write a signal at that exact call site, where the intent is already unambiguous.
Decay by shelf life Cheap The columns exist and the backfill is a regex over 384 strings. The one thing to change from the old design: the original deleted expired rows nightly by cron. Decay to low confidence, never to absent — a deleted onion and an onion you are unsure about are different states, and only one of them can be shown to the user for correction.
Resync by fridge photo Mostly there POST /rest/ingredients/extract already returns catalogue ingredient ids with a matchType of exact_name | alias | vector | created. One call replaces the inventory wholesale. Two honest gaps: confidence is reported per response, not per item, so you cannot tell which of twelve items was the shaky one; and there are no quantities at all — it answers “what”, never “how much”.
Rank, don't filter New The wall above. This is the single largest piece of genuinely new work in the whole direction.
One risk that a planner amplifies. CLAUDE.md is explicit that an unclassified ingredient makes its dish UNKNOWN for dietary purposes and silently withdraws it from every dietary filter and from recipe_display_tags, with nothing to report it. Today that costs one missing card. In a week plan it costs a slot, and the user sees a plan that quietly refuses to include anything they can eat on Thursday. Same for the ingredient-identity split (brussels_sprout vs brussel_sprout) — a fridge item on the losing side of a split simply never matches. Both are existing, known, logged issues; the planner is what makes them visible.

The week

The smallest honest version, and why it is not a menu

Given that menus are shared, deduplicated, main-anchored community rows, a plan must not be one. The good news is that the right model is already demonstrated in this schema.

The precedent is 20260819000001_shopping_list_menu_group.sql, and its header argues the case better than I can: it added menu_main_recipe_id and menu_title as columns on rows that already existed rather than creating a shopping_list_groups table — “zero new rows for a feature that is, from the database's point of view, a GROUP BY.”

A week is the same move, one level up. A plan is an annotation grouping shopping-list rows, not a new entity that owns them:

Slots, not dates

I would not put a date on it in v1, and this is the deviation argument rather than a shortcut. A calendar creates the failure it is meant to prevent. Cook Thursday's dish on Tuesday and a dated plan is now wrong in two places and needs reconciling; an ordered set of five dishes does not notice, because nothing asserted which day anything belonged to. plan_slot as a position gives you ordering for display and costs nothing when reality disagrees.

Deviation is free, by construction

This falls out of the model rather than needing to be designed. A plan is a set of references; a shopping_lists row is already independently deletable and the client already handles removing one. Swapping day 3 is a delete and an insert. Nothing cascades, nothing is invalidated, and there is no adherence state to be wrong about — which is the property you wanted, arrived at by not building the thing that would have broken it.

One consequence to accept deliberately: if a plan is only an annotation, then deleting the last dish deletes the plan. That is correct. A plan with nothing in it is not a plan the user is failing at, it is a plan they abandoned, and keeping the husk around to report emptiness is how planners start nagging.

The one thing that genuinely degrades at weekly scale

Amounts are listed per dish, never summed. That is a deliberate decision with a good argument behind it — “Two dishes wanting 200 ml cream and 1 tbsp cream have no common unit to add in, and a total that silently drops one of them is worse than two lines.” At three courses, 400 ml + 1 tbsp is genuinely the honest answer.

Three courses — reads well
Cream 200 ml + 1 tbsp Onion 1 + 2

Two contributions, both legible at a glance. You can hold this in your head at the counter.

Twelve dishes — falls over
Onion 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 + 3 Olive oil 2 tbsp + 1 tbsp + 60 ml + 1 tbsp + 2 tbsp

The rule that was honest at three is unreadable at twelve. This is the one place the existing code needs real work for a week rather than a parameter.

The fix is narrow and does not overturn the decision: sum within a unit family, list across families. g/kg and ml/l collapse; 2 tbsp + 60 ml stays two terms, because that is still the honest answer. Countables collapse to a number. The units table exists and the client already has formatScaledAmount as the single place an amount becomes text, so this lands in one function with the existing rule intact as the fallback.

Opinion

Is this a better anchor than compose?

Yes — and the repo has already written down why compose failed, in enough detail that you do not have to take my word for it.

From core/access/constants/features.ts, on compose: it “was the only paid entry here until 2026-08-26, which was the problem: a subscription that unlocks one surface, several taps deep, is not one anybody reaches.” That is your own diagnosis, dated, and it generalises to exactly the argument you are making — recurrence beats occasion.

How the tiers sit today, and where a planner would land:

TierHolds todayWhere a planner belongs
guestBrowse; personalize — moved here today, 2026-09-02Seeing that planning exists. Nothing more.
accountsave — favourites, collections, shopping lists, saved menusThe plan itself and its list. This is the user's own data and it is already free at this tier. Do not move it.
subscriptionEvery model call — generate, suggest, chat, extract, compose, import…Filling the plan for you. The selection, the ranking, the fridge photo. Anything that runs a model.

That split is not a compromise, it is the honest line and it matches the existing rule (“if a model runs, it is paid”) without amendment. It also gives the paywall the placement the file says converts: it lands on somebody who has already assembled part of a week by hand and is being offered the rest.

The cost caveat, which is real. shopping_lists.recipe_id is a foreign key to recipes, so only generated recipes can go on a list — a suggestion cannot. You already hit this with menus and solved it with usePendingShoppingListStore (“add this course too, once it exists”), which is directly reusable. But it means a five-dish plan of catalogue suggestions is five promote calls before the shopping list is complete. Compose is occasional; a week recurs — which is the whole argument for it as an anchor and simultaneously the whole risk. Price it against someone planning five dishes every week, not against someone composing a dinner party twice a year.

Which is an argument for catalogue-first planning, not just a cost note. A plan assembled from find_recipes hits costs nothing to fill, because those recipes already exist. Generation is the fallback for slots the catalogue cannot fill. That is better product and cheaper, and it is the same instinct as compose's retrieval-before-generation, which turned a multi-second paid stream into ~40 ms.

Cold start

The honest first week

A new user has no fridge, no history, no cooked dishes, and no saved meals. That is the state every user is in exactly once, and it is the state the feature is most likely to be judged in.

What they do have on day one is more than it sounds: a catalogue with dietary filtering, difficulty preference, cuisine and total_time_minutes banded into quick/moderate/long; the near-miss tier; and the onboarding answers, which as of today are guest-tier and so are available before anyone signs in.

So the honest first-week plan is a catalogue plan — five real dishes that fit the diet, the skill level and the weeknight time band, chosen partly for what they share so the shopping list is short. Notably that needs no fridge and no history. The overlap ranking it wants is the same function the fridge needs, just seeded from the plan's own dishes instead of an inventory.

The fridge photo is the right first-run invitation because it is this app's signature interaction and it converts an empty inventory into a full one in a single tap. But it is extract, which is subscription-tier — so on day one, for a non-payer, it is a paywall. That is defensible, but it means the free cold start must be genuinely good without it, and the catalogue plan above is what has to carry that.

The failure to avoid. The personalize reversal earlier today put the argument well: “Gates convert in proportion to the investment already made, and this one fires before any exists.” A planner that asks a first-run user to fill a fridge before it will show them anything fires exactly that gate. The plan should exist and be useful before the fridge does — which the ordering below reflects.

Verdict

Is the direction wrong for this app?

No. But the shape of the bet is different from the one implied by the question, in two ways worth stating plainly.

The week is nearly free and the fridge is the product. You have an ingredient-id-keyed, aisle-grouped, servings-scaled, offline-capable aggregated shopping list that already merges an arbitrary number of recipes. The planner is a label on top of it. If the value is “one shopping list for the week”, you are two columns and a filter away, and you should find that out in a fortnight rather than a quarter.

The fridge's cost is in retrieval, not storage. Everyone assumes pantry inventory is expensive because maintaining it is a UX problem — and your forgiving design is the right answer to that, and it is buildable, with the decay substrate already in the schema and the observed-signal pattern already proven in profile_taste_signals. The cost is elsewhere: this database can filter by ingredient and cannot rank by ingredient, and every existing path assumes the former. That is real work, it is unavoidable, and it is the thing to scope carefully.

The one caution I would hold onto: a plan is a promise about the future, and this app has been careful — in the slot ledger, in onAdmit, in withdrawing nothing that was never announced — not to show the user anything it cannot keep. A weekly plan is the largest promise the product would have made. The forgiving fridge and the undated slot are both ways of making it a smaller one, which is why they are the right instincts, and why I would build the version that promises least first.
