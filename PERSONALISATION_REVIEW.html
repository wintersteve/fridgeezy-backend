Fridgeezy · personalisation & recipe matching · 30 Aug 2026

The dishes that almost fit

You have eight stores of personal data and three of them reach a query. The rest are written faithfully and read by nobody. Before adding anything, the honest finding is that personalisation here is not missing — it is dropped on the floor in three specific places, and switched off in a fourth.

Your near-miss idea is right, and it is buildable today from data that already exists — I measured it. But the number that decides whether a near-miss is a good idea is not how close the dish is. Eighteen catalogue recipes are one ingredient away from vegan; most of them are one ingredient away because that ingredient is the dish. Bak Kut Teh minus the pork ribs is not a vegan Bak Kut Teh.

02 · Where it is dropped

Three leaks. None of them needs new data.

These are the highest-value work in this document and none of them is a feature. Each is a place where a preference the user stated, and the app already loaded into memory, does not reach the query.

Leak 1 — the home feed ignores your diet

useBaseRecipeFilters returns { difficulty, blacklist }. The same store holds restrictions, persisted across restarts and rehydrated from profile_dietary_preferences on every home-screen focus — and the home feed does not pass it.

The comment in home-feed.tsx says "Only the user's own preferences apply — their skill level, and the ingredients they never want to see." It lists two of the three. A vegan's home feed is the entire catalogue.

Fix: add tags: restrictions.map(r => r.id) to useBaseRecipeFilters. One line, client-only, no tarball rebuild. Guard it on hasHydrated — the store already exposes that for exactly this class of read.

Leak 2 — chat honours your diet only when it writes a dish, never when it finds one

searchRecipeSuggestions takes dietaryRestrictions and uses it in exactly two places: streamSingleSuggestion and generateSuggestionsStream. Both are stage 3. Stages 1a (exact name), 1b (vector), 1c (find_recipes) and 2 (suggestions) — every path that returns a dish that already exists — never see it.

And retrieval wins by design — the whole point of the ladder is to return a catalogue hit before paying for a generation. So the dish the model would have written is vegan, and the dish you actually get is Carbonara. Note the asymmetry that makes this hard to spot: blacklist is forwarded to stage 1c, so half the constraint machinery looks right.

Fix: resolve the diet names to dietary_rules.diet_canonical_id once, pass the tag ids into findCatalogueRecipes (which already calls find_recipes, which already takes tags), and post-filter 1a/1b/2 against recipe_dietary. Server-only — no schema change, no tarball rebuild.

Leak 3 — ranking knows your skill level and nothing else

find_recipes ends on order by source, difficulty_preference_rank, favourite_count desc, name, id. Skill level is the only per-reader term. favourite_count is everyone's favourites — with one favourite in the database it currently orders nothing, and at scale it orders by popularity, which is the opposite of personal.

This is the correct place for revealed preference to enter, and the correct way for it to enter: as an ORDER BY term below the restriction tiers, never as a WHERE clause. The existing comment already makes exactly this argument for why likes sit below difficulty — "skill level is a stated preference and a like count is inferred taste". Extend the principle rather than inventing one.

All three fixes together are perhaps a day. They need no new table, no new model call, and no data that does not exist. They also work on the first launch for anybody who ticked a box in onboarding — which is the only personalisation in this document with no cold-start problem at all.

The taste-profile review is due 2026-09-13. The data already answers it.

§11 predicted this precisely — "a feature that fires only on harder is thin" — and the outcome is worse than thin. The two qualifying signals are the same axis pointing in both directions. One profile has asked to make things harder three times and easier twice. Fed to readStandingPreferences, that yields the instruction "take the technique further, with more refined method; keep the technique plain and the step count low", joined by a semicolon, in that order, because it sorts by count.

That is not a calibration problem. Difficulty is not a standing preference at all — it is a per-dish decision, and the app already has a first-class place to record it (profile_settings.difficulty, which the user sets deliberately and which already ranks every feed). Recording it as a taste signal was the design error, not the threshold.

The other two kinds behave exactly as §11 feared and for the reason it gave. modification stores canonicalised free text, so use_store_bought_curry_paste_instead_of — truncated mid-word at 40 characters — is a key nothing will ever collide with. substitution stores ingredient names and will accumulate, but two rows at one occurrence each after two weeks of single-user testing is not evidence of anything yet.

Prompt history: right table, wrong question

29 rows, one profile. The contents are unambiguous:

"Give me a pad kraw pao recipe"        ×4 consecutive
"Give me a japanese curry recipe"      ×3 (two spellings, one typo)
"Give me a carbonara recipe"           ×2
"Test"                                 ×2
"It's still not beef"
"Give me a laksa recipe Michelin leebl"

These are dish requests, not preference statements. Nobody has typed "I don't like coriander" and nobody will — that is what the blacklist screen is for. So any design that reads prompts as a preference log needs an LLM to extract preferences that are not there.

But there is a real signal in that list, and it is not the one INGREDIENT_AFFINITY.md §4.4 ruled out. That analysis correctly rejected prompts as an ingredient-affinity source (wrong granularity — it re-derives the catalogue's own affinity weighted by request frequency). Cuisine affinity is a different claim. Of the 21 non-test prompts, 14 name a Thai, Japanese, Korean, Chinese or Malaysian dish. That resolves without any NLP — prompt → dish name → catalogue row → cuisine tag — and cuisine is a taxonomy the app already ranks, filters and renders. It is the one thing prompt history can honestly say about a person today.

The threshold for acting on it is not row count, it is profile count. Cuisine affinity from one user's 29 prompts is a mirror, not a model. Build the read as a view now if you like — it costs nothing and it is checkable — but do not put it on a surface until there are enough profiles that being wrong about one of them is a rounding error rather than the entire experience.

What "almost fits" means concretely

find_recipes narrows on three hard predicates, and each one has a natural, countable relaxation:

PredicateHard form (today)Near-miss form
blacklist Recipe contains none of the excluded ids. Contains exactly one. The offending ingredient is known by name, and decideReuse / adaptRecipeForBlacklist already do the adaptation on the promote path. Only the retrieval half is new.
tags (derivable diet) recipe_dietary is a not exists over ingredients carrying a forbidden property. Invert it and count. One blocker → the dish is one swap from qualifying, and the query returns which ingredient it is. This is the whole feature and it costs a GROUP BY.
ingredients Recipe contains all requested ids. Contains n−1. Weakest of the three: "you have four of the five" is a shopping-list observation, not an adaptation, and it competes with a feature INGREDIENT_AFFINITY.md §4.1 already deferred with numbers.

05 · The three ways this goes wrong

Treated as constraints on the design, not as caveats after it.

Failure 1 · the catalogue feels smaller than it is

The rule: personalisation may only ever ADD to the tail, or REORDER below the fold. It may never remove a row from the head.

Two kinds of preference are being conflated and they have opposite semantics. A restriction — diet, allergy, blacklist — is a correctness constraint and belongs in the WHERE clause; someone who cannot eat gluten is not being narrowed, they are being served. A taste — cuisine affinity, difficulty, "you always cook Thai" — is inferred, and belongs only in the ORDER BY, below the restriction tiers. find_recipes already draws this line for likes; extend it rather than replacing it.

The near-miss tier is safe on this axis by construction: it is strictly additive. It shows dishes the current filters reject. And the escape hatch already exists — find_recipes returns total_recipes and facets over the whole match, so a header can honestly say "51 vegan dishes · 17 of them with one change" instead of silently presenting a shorter list.

Failure 2 · presumption

The rule: declared beats revealed, always, and revealed needs repetition before it counts.

The taste-signal design got this right — TASTE_SIGNAL_MIN_OCCURRENCES = 2 exists because "make this one vegetarian" is a fact about a dinner party. Keep that rule and apply it to anything new: cuisine affinity from a single request is noise, and the substitution corpus is explicitly not being read until it has volume.

The concrete guard for the near-miss tier: it fires off the user's declared diet and blacklist only. It must not fire off an inferred preference, because the whole surface says "this dish does not meet a constraint you set" — and that sentence is false if nobody set one.

Failure 3 · a label that is a claim

The rule: if you cannot print the reason in six words, you may not print the badge.

"Personalised for you" is a marketing sentence. "Uses soy sauce — we'd use tamari" is a falsifiable one, and the reader can tell instantly whether it is right. Every idea in this document either produces a printable reason or does not ship. Cuisine affinity passes ("You cook a lot of Thai"). Near-miss passes ("one swap: parmesan"). Anything derived from an LLM reading your prompt history does not, which is a large part of why it is ranked last.

This also settles the visible-and-correctable question, and I would argue it strongly: the reason should be a control, not a caption. Tapping "You cook a lot of Thai" should open the thing that decides it, and it should be possible to say no I don't. profile_taste_signals already grants the owner delete and deliberately no insert/update for exactly this — the capability exists and only the screen is missing. Turn that screen on whether or not any of the rest of this ships; §11 already names it as the one undeferrable item, and it becomes more urgent, not less, the moment anything starts acting on inferred data.

08 · The smallest version worth shipping

Two changes, one of which is a bug fix.

  1. The two diet leaks. One line in useBaseRecipeFilters to pass restrictions as tag ids; and in searchRecipeSuggestions, resolve the diet names once and forward them to findCatalogueRecipes plus a recipe_dietary post-filter on stages 1a/1b/2. No migration, no schema, no tarball rebuild, no model call. This is where I would stop and ship.
  2. One SQL function, one rail. find_near_miss_recipes(p_diets, p_blacklist, …) — same shape and same visibility discipline as menu_pairings_for_recipe, returning the recipe plus the single blocking ingredient. The client renders a "Close, with one change" section under the exact results and above the generate boundary, with the blocker named on each card. Nothing is adapted yet — tapping a card opens the existing recipe with the blocker highlighted.
  3. Then, and only if the rail is tapped: the adaptation gate and the write into recipe_variants. That is the expensive half and it is the half that can wait for a number.

What that buys, on today's catalogue: a vegan search goes from 13 results to 13 + 18 marked ones; gluten free from 21 to 21 + 24. Both currently fall through to a paid generation the moment the reader scrolls, so the near-miss rail also displaces spend rather than only adding a surface.

Ship risk. Step 1 is a filter that should always have been applied — the only way it regresses is by making a result set smaller, which is what a restriction is for. Step 2 is a read-only function feeding an additive section; if it disappoints, drop the function and the section and nothing else notices. Step 3 is the only one that writes, and it writes into a family shape that already exists and already has a merge guard.

One caveat on step 2 worth knowing before you start. A new RPC means regenerating database.types.ts, which means rebuilding and repacking @fridgeezy/types into the client's vendor/ — the manual step CLAUDE.md warns is easy to forget, and a bare npm install will not pick it up. If you want step 2 with no cross-repo dance, type the RPC response locally in the client for the first iteration and fold it into the generated types at the next scheduled refresh.
